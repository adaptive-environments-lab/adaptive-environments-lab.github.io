Thank you very much for the chance to come back to you with our revised submission.

First, we want to be clear that our vision for the Adaptive Environments Lab at UVA can and should remain unchanged.

However, you gave us very direct feedback: you wanted to see the first concrete step. Our answer is the Rivanna Project — a summer teaching prototype for **AI-enabled environmental design.**

This project is designed to deliver what we are calling a **teaching chassis** — a tested, portable AEL course module, built this summer, that Brad and I will immediately put to work this fall and spring in studio courses in the School of Architecture, open to students from Public Health, Environmental Science, and other disciplines.

Everything else in the larger AEL vision — research, thought leadership — grows from here.

We know you've read the proposal, so we're not going to walk you through it again. But in the next few minutes, we want to outline what the Rivanna Project is designed to deliver, why it's the necessary first step, and why we're excited to get started this summer. Brad.

## BRAD — Why This, Why Now (≈2:25)

Thanks, Matt. So why did we choose a teaching prototype as the first step for the Adaptive Environments Lab? Because teaching AI-enabled environmental design is a major step change, and the window to figure out how to do it is both urgent and narrow. Three things have to be worked out before the AEL and the new era of design we envision can begin — and none of them can be worked out on a whiteboard.

**First, the AEL tech stack.** Drones, compute, and the genuinely unsexy infrastructure to run a class that looks nothing like a normal studio. You can see exactly what we're asking for in the budget. We need a summer to buy it, fire it up, break it, and learn what the real operating model is.

**Second, a new pedagogy — and the binding constraint is speed.** The beating heart of the design labs the AEL will teach isn't the drones. It's the generative AI models — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini — that now let a student process a point cloud overnight that used to take a specialized team months. Those models are changing so fast that any course optimized to a specific model is obsolete before the semester ends. The pedagogy has to teach something underneath all of that.

**Third — a compelling narrative about AI and the future of design for students — and this is the heart of why we call this the *Adaptive* Environments Lab.** What we actually have to teach is a shift in how students think about what a designer can take on. Think at a system level. Take on audacious problems — health, environmental sustainability at a regional scale — that a previous generation of designers would not have attempted. *That* is what survives the next model release.

We need to show students that generative AI does not mean that design gets automated. It is the opposite. Generative AI is rapidly democratizing capabilities that used to live only inside well-funded research teams. But this isn't the current mindset or belief among many students. Matt and I are both seeing, in the students we teach today, a real divide between what is now possible and how they are thinking about it. Many are intimidated. Some are anxious about agency and authorship. Some are inclined simply not to engage — and honestly, that is the part that worries us most. Because it is not an option not to engage. Our students need to take an active role in shaping their relationship with this technology. And this attitudinal shift among students, faculty, and other stakeholders is the most important thing we want the AEL to catalyze. This summer prototype is how we design a course that we hope gets that shift started — not by telling students that the future is 100% certain, but by giving them the knowledge, skills, and mindset to jump in, learn by doing, and be leaders in this new era.

That is what the Rivanna summer is for, and that is why we chose teaching as the place the Adaptive Environments Lab has to begin.
